Topic 01 of 11

What is Artificial Intelligence?

Everyone uses the word. Very few can define it in a way that survives a follow-up question. Start here, because every other topic in this module depends on getting this one right.

Artificial Intelligence is software that performs tasks which normally require human intelligence — understanding language, recognising patterns, making a judgement, or producing something new. That is the textbook definition, and on its own it is not very useful. The part that matters for your career is the shift hiding underneath it.

The inversion that changed everything

For roughly fifty years, business software worked exactly one way. A human thought through every rule, and a programmer wrote those rules down. If a situation was not anticipated, the software could not handle it — it would error, or quietly do the wrong thing.

Your payroll system knows that PF is 12% because a person typed 12 into a configuration screen. It does not know what PF is, why it exists, or that the rate might change. It is following an instruction, precisely and blindly.

AI inverts this relationship. Instead of being given the rules, the system is given examples and derives the rules itself. This matters because there are enormous categories of business problem where nobody can write the rule down.

Rules can be written

Traditional software handles this

  1. If an invoice is unpaid after 30 days, flag it
  2. PF is 12% of basic salary
  3. Reorder when stock falls below 100 units
  4. Apply 18% GST to this product category
Rules cannot be written

This is where AI earns its place

  1. Is this customer email frustrated, or just blunt?
  2. What is this enquiry actually asking for?
  3. Summarise this 40-minute meeting
  4. Does this resume match this job?

Nobody can write the rule for "this customer sounds frustrated". There are thousands of ways to express frustration in English, more in Hindi, and more again in the Hinglish your customers actually write. But show a system enough examples and it learns the pattern — including the subtle cases a rulebook would never have covered.

Key idea

Traditional software is told what to do. AI is shown what good looks like and works out the rest. That is why AI can absorb messy, unpredictable human input — and also why it is never 100% certain, which becomes the central theme of Topic 07.

Narrow AI vs General AI

Every AI system in commercial use today is Narrow AI: extremely capable inside a defined range of tasks, and completely blank outside it. A model that writes excellent sales emails has no awareness that it is doing sales, no goals of its own, and no memory of you between conversations unless a product has been specifically built to store one.

General AI — a system with human-level ability across any domain, with genuine understanding and its own intent — does not exist. It is a research ambition, not something you can procure. When a vendor's pitch starts to sound like general AI, that is marketing, and you should price it accordingly.

Why AI became useful now, and not in 1995

The field dates from the 1950s, and it spent decades being genuinely disappointing. Four things changed in the last ten years:

What changedWhy it mattered
DataDigitisation produced the enormous text and image collections these systems learn from. No data, no patterns to learn.
ComputeGPUs made it economically possible to train on that data in months rather than centuries.
ArchitectureThe transformer design, published in 2017, let models hold long stretches of language in view at once instead of processing word by word.
AccessFrom late 2022, chat interfaces removed the need to be a programmer. A sales manager could use a frontier model directly, with no IT project.

That last row is the one that affects you. The technology stopped being something a company bought and became something an individual employee could simply start using — which is exactly why the skill gap between two people in the same role is now so visible.

Career framing

AI is not a product you buy — it is a capability you insert into a process. Companies are not hiring people who can name AI tools; that list changes every quarter. They are hiring people who can look at an existing business process, find the step that is slow because a human is reading or writing something, and redesign it. Every module in this program trains that one skill.

★

Take away: AI learns rules from examples instead of being given them. That is why it handles messy human input — and why its answers are probabilities, never guarantees.

Topic 02 of 11

AI vs Machine Learning vs Deep Learning

Three terms, used interchangeably by almost everybody, that actually sit inside one another. Placing them correctly is a standard interview question — and getting it wrong signals that you learned AI from headlines.

They nest. Generative AI is a specific use of deep learning, which is a specific kind of machine learning, which is one route to AI.

How machine learning actually learns

Strip away the mathematics and the process is simple. You collect examples where you already know the answer — say, 5,000 past leads and whether each one eventually bought. You show the system the inputs together with the known outcomes. It adjusts its internal weights until its predictions match those outcomes reasonably well. Then you give it a lead it has never seen, and it predicts.

1 · Training dataPast examples where the answer is already known.
→
2 · TrainingThe model adjusts itself until its predictions match the known answers.
→
3 · PredictionIt is given new, unseen input and produces an answer.

This is why data quality decides model quality. If your past lead records are incomplete, or your labels were applied inconsistently, the model faithfully learns that mess. There is no clever algorithm that repairs bad training data — a point Module 6 returns to in detail.

The distinction that actually matters

Classical machine learning needs a human to decide what the model should look at. To predict lead quality, somebody must decide that company size, industry and budget are the useful signals. That step is called feature engineering, and it puts a ceiling on the system: it can only be as insightful as the person choosing the features.

Deep learning removes that ceiling. Given enough examples, a multi-layer neural network works out for itself which signals matter — including combinations no human would have thought to specify. That is precisely why deep learning took over everything involving language, images and audio. Nobody can list the features that make a sentence sound angry. A network trained on enough angry sentences finds them.

TermWhat it isHow it worksBusiness example
Artificial Intelligence The overall goal — machines performing tasks that need intelligence Any technique at all, including hand-written rules A chatbot that routes a customer to the right department
Machine Learning A method of achieving AI by learning from data instead of being programmed Humans choose the features; the model learns the weights Predicting which leads are likely to convert, from past sales data
Deep Learning Machine learning using neural networks with many layers The model discovers the features itself — no feature engineering Reading a scanned purchase order and extracting the line items
Generative AI Deep learning models that produce new content Predicts the most probable next piece of content, repeatedly Drafting a proposal, summarising a meeting, writing a job description
Say it like this in an interview

"AI is the objective. Machine learning is the main way we get there — the system learns patterns from data rather than following written rules. Deep learning is machine learning with multi-layer neural networks, which is what finally made language and images work. Generative AI is deep learning applied to creating new content."

★

Take away: They nest, they do not compete. AI is the goal, ML is the method, deep learning is the breakthrough, generative AI is the application you use every day.

Topic 03 of 11

What is Generative AI?

This is the change that pulled AI out of the data science team and put it on every desk in the company — including, shortly, yours.

Until around 2020, virtually all commercial AI was predictive. You handed it something that already existed and it told you something about it: is this transaction fraudulent, will this customer churn, does this resume match, what is in this photograph. Genuinely useful — but the output was always a label, a score or a number.

Generative AI creates something that did not exist before. You describe what you need and it produces the artefact itself: the email, the summary, the analysis, the code, the image, the structured record.

Predictive AIGenerative AI
Question it answers"What is this?" / "What will happen?""Make me this."
OutputA category, score or numberNew content — text, image, code, audio
ExampleThis lead scores 82 out of 100Here is the follow-up email for this lead
Who used itData teams, through a projectEveryone, directly, from day one
Setup neededMonths of data workOpen a browser tab

Why this changed business roles, not just IT

Look honestly at what a marketing executive, an HR generalist, a sales manager or a business analyst produces in a working day. Emails. Job descriptions. Campaign copy. Meeting notes. Proposals. Comparison sheets. Status reports. Almost all professional output is content — and content is precisely what generative AI makes.

Predictive AI made data scientists more productive. Generative AI makes everyone else more productive. That is the whole reason your employer now expects fluency in it regardless of your function, and the reason this program exists at all.

DepartmentWhat generative AI produces
SalesOutreach emails, call preparation notes, objection responses, proposal drafts, meeting summaries
MarketingCampaign concepts, ad copy, social posts, customer personas, content calendars
HRJob descriptions, interview questions, candidate summaries, onboarding material
FinanceVariance explanations, management commentary, dashboard narratives
OperationsProcess documentation, SOPs, supplier comparison notes, incident summaries
Key idea

Generative AI does not remove the need for judgement — it removes the cost of the first draft. The value you add shifts from producing the document to deciding whether the document is right. That shift is what the rest of this module prepares you for.

★

Take away: Predictive AI describes what exists; generative AI creates what does not. Because business output is mostly content, this is the version that reaches every role.

Topic 04 of 11

How a ChatGPT-type system actually works

You do not need the mathematics. You do need an accurate mental model — because nearly every expensive mistake people make with AI comes from holding the wrong one.

LLM stands for Large Language Model. "Large" refers to the number of internal parameters — the adjustable values the model learned during training. Frontier models have hundreds of billions of them. That scale is why the behaviour feels qualitatively different from older systems, but the underlying operation is remarkably simple.

It predicts the next piece of text. That is genuinely all.

An LLM breaks language into tokens — roughly, word fragments. "Understanding" might be two tokens, "ERP" might be one, a comma is one. Given every token so far, the model calculates which token is most likely to come next, appends it, and repeats the whole calculation. A 400-word email is that same prediction running several hundred times in sequence.

The reason this produces something coherent rather than nonsense is scale. Trained on an enormous body of human writing, the task "predict what comes next" forces the model to internalise grammar, tone, document structure, domain conventions and a great deal of factual association — because every one of those things shapes which word genuinely comes next.

The three stages of training

1 · Pre-trainingReads a vast body of text and learns language, structure and world patterns. Enormously expensive, done once.
→
2 · Fine-tuningTrained on example instructions with good answers, so it responds to requests instead of merely continuing text.
→
3 · AlignmentHumans rate competing answers; the model is tuned toward the ones people rated helpful, safe and well formatted.

Stage 3 explains a great deal about how the model behaves. It was rewarded for answers people rated highly. Confidence was rewarded, because confident answers read better. Accuracy was rewarded only when the human rater happened to know the truth. Hold on to that — it is the direct cause of everything in Topic 07.

The context window is its only memory

Everything the model can see at once — your instruction, the document you pasted, the conversation so far — sits inside a fixed-size context window. Within that window it has excellent access. Outside it, the information simply does not exist for the model.

ConsequenceWhat it means in practice
It knows nothing about your companyIt has never seen your price list, your policies or your customers. If you do not paste it in, it will invent it.
Long documents must be broken upPast a certain length the model is working with only part of your document — often without saying so.
A new chat starts blankNothing carries over between conversations unless the product was explicitly built to store it.
Earlier turns can fall outIn a very long conversation, the beginning may drop out of the window and be forgotten mid-discussion.

Why the same question gives different answers

The model does not always take the single most likely next token; it samples from a probability distribution. This is deliberate — always taking the top choice produces flat, repetitive writing. The side effect is that identical prompts can produce different output on different days.

This is normal and expected behaviour, not a fault. But it has a hard consequence: an LLM must never sit in a process that requires a byte-identical result every single time. Topic 05 turns that into a decision rule you can apply.

The single most important sentence in this module

An LLM generates text that is statistically plausible. It is not looking anything up, and it has no internal test for truth. Fluent and correct feel completely identical coming out of it. Verification is your job, not the model's.

★

Take away: An LLM predicts the next token from what is in its context window. It does not retrieve facts, and it cannot tell when it is wrong.

Topic 05 of 11

Generative AI vs traditional software

Both are software, and that is where the similarity stops. Confusing the two is the most expensive mistake a business makes with AI, so this comparison is worth learning properly.

Traditional softwareGenerative AI
LogicRules written by a humanPatterns learned from data
Same input twiceIdentical output, alwaysMay differ slightly each time
AccuracyExactApproximate, usually very good
Unexpected inputRejects it or throws an errorAttempts an answer anyway
How it failsLoudly — it crashes or refusesQuietly — a confident wrong answer
Handles ambiguityNoYes, this is its core strength
TestingPass or failJudged on quality, needs sampling and review
Changing behaviourEdit code, test, redeployChange the instruction, immediate
Audit trailEvery step traceableReasoning is not reliably reconstructable

Row five is the one that costs companies real money. When a conventional system fails you know immediately — a screen errors, a batch job aborts, somebody raises a ticket. When an LLM fails, you receive a well-written paragraph with a wrong number in it, formatted exactly like the paragraphs that were right. Nothing alerts you. It reaches the customer.

A decision rule you can apply from day one

Use traditional software

When the answer must be exactly right, every time

  1. Salary, PF and TDS calculation
  2. GST computation and return filing
  3. Accounting entries and ledgers
  4. Inventory quantity on hand
  5. Invoice totals and payment reconciliation
  6. Anything with a legal or audit consequence
Use generative AI

When the task involves language, judgement or variety

  1. Summarising a long email thread
  2. Drafting proposals and outreach
  3. Categorising unstructured enquiries
  4. Extracting fields from messy text
  5. Explaining what a report implies
  6. Generating options to choose between

The pattern that beats both

The strongest business systems do not choose. They use AI for the part that is unstructured and human, and conventional software for the part that must be exact and auditable.

1 · Messy inputAn email, a scanned PO, a resume, a complaint — unstructured human text.
→
2 · AI reads itExtracts the fields: company, quantity, amount, date, requirement.
→
3 · Human checksConfirms the extraction, especially anything with a rupee value attached.
→
4 · System computesERP or CRM does the exact, auditable work with clean structured data.

Learn this shape now. Module 8 builds it out properly against ERP and CRM, and most real AI projects in Indian mid-market companies are some version of it.

★

Take away: Exactness and accountability belong to conventional software. Language, ambiguity and judgement belong to AI. Great systems use AI to feed clean data into software.

Topic 06 of 11

AI capabilities and limitations

Professional competence with AI is mostly knowing where the boundary sits. Underestimate it and you waste hours. Overestimate it and you send a customer something wrong.

What it is genuinely excellent at

  • Summarising — a 30-page report, a 60-message thread, an hour of meeting notes, into what actually matters.
  • Drafting — the first 80% of any document, in seconds rather than an hour.
  • Rewriting — changing tone, length, language or audience while preserving meaning.
  • Structuring the unstructured — turning a rambling email into clean fields. This is the single highest-value business use of the technology.
  • Classifying at volume — sorting enquiries, tickets or feedback into consistent categories, at a scale no human will sustain.
  • Explaining — making a technical or financial concept clear for a specific audience.
  • Generating options — twelve subject lines, six objection responses, five campaign angles, so you can choose rather than invent.
  • Translating — including into and out of Indian languages, for customer communication.

Where it is weak, and what you do about it

LimitationWhat goes wrongHow you work around it
No live knowledgeTraining has a cut-off date; recent events are unknown or guessed atUse a tool with search, or paste the current information in yourself
No company knowledgeIt invents your pricing, policies, modules and client namesAlways supply the real document inside the prompt
Arithmetic at scaleMulti-step calculations drift; totals look plausible but do not add upLet it set up the logic, then compute in Excel and verify
Not a source of truthStatistics, citations and legal clauses can be fabricated wholesaleVerify every figure against a real source before it leaves your desk
No accountabilityThe model cannot own a decision or its consequencesA named human signs off anything that leaves the company
Confidentiality riskData pasted into a public tool may leave your controlFollow company policy; never paste customer, salary or contract data into public tools
OverconfidenceIt rarely says "I do not know" unless you explicitly allow it toInstruct it to flag uncertainty and to ask rather than guess
The 80% principle

Treat AI output as a strong first draft from a fast, exceptionally well-read intern who has never met your customers and will not admit uncertainty. The last 20% — the facts, the numbers, the relationship, the judgement — is exactly where you earn your salary. Skip it and you have added no value at all, only risk.

★

Take away: Excellent at language, structure and volume. Unreliable on current facts, exact arithmetic and anything requiring accountability.

Topic 07 of 11

Hallucination, bias and inaccurate information

This is the topic that separates someone who is employable with AI from someone who is a liability with it. Read this one twice.

Hallucination

A hallucination is output that is fluent, confident, well formatted and factually false. It is not a bug awaiting a patch — it follows directly from the mechanism in Topic 04. The model produces the most plausible continuation. When it does not hold the fact, the most plausible continuation is still a fact-shaped sentence, so it writes one, in exactly the same assured tone it uses when correct.

What it looks like in an actual workplace:

  • Invented figures — "the Indian ERP market grew 23.4% in 2024", stated with total confidence and no basis whatsoever.
  • Fake references — a plausible report title from a real consulting firm that was never published.
  • Fabricated product detail — modules, features or integrations your product does not have, written into a proposal.
  • Wrong attribution — a real quotation assigned to the wrong person or company.
  • Confident compliance claims — a clause of law or a GST rule stated crisply and incorrectly.
Why this is a business risk, not a technical curiosity

A hallucinated number in a client proposal is a credibility loss. A hallucinated compliance statement is legal exposure. A hallucinated specification in a tender may be a commitment your company has to honour at its own cost. Lawyers in several countries have been formally sanctioned for filing AI-generated case citations that did not exist — they did not check, because the output looked professional.

Bias

The model learned from human-written text, so it absorbed human patterns — including the unfair ones. Bias shows up as systematically different treatment of similar people or cases:

  • Resume screening that quietly favours certain colleges, cities, surnames or career paths.
  • Job descriptions written in language that measurably skews who applies.
  • Performance summaries that describe identical behaviour differently by gender.
  • Default assumptions — that a "software engineer" is male, that a strong candidate studied in a metro.

Bias is harder to catch than hallucination, because each individual output looks entirely reasonable. You only see it in aggregate — which is why an AI-assisted process needs auditing, not just spot-checking. Module 5 takes this much further in a recruitment setting, where the legal and ethical stakes are highest.

Your verification playbook

PracticeWhat it means in daily work
Ground itPaste the real source document instead of relying on the model's memory. A model working from your text hallucinates far less than one working from recall.
Check every numberTreat all figures, dates, percentages and names as unverified until you have seen them in a real source.
Demand checkable sourcesAsk for references — then actually open them. An unopened citation is worth nothing at all.
Ask it to challenge itself"What in this answer are you least confident about, and what would make it wrong?" surfaces weak claims quickly.
Give it permission to not know"If you are not sure, say so and ask me" converts silent invention into a question you can answer.
Scale scrutiny to stakesAn internal brainstorm needs a glance. A customer proposal, an offer letter, or anything with a rupee figure needs line-by-line checking.
Protect confidential dataNo salary data, customer records, contracts or unreleased plans in public tools.
Keep a human accountableEvery AI-assisted output that leaves the company has a named person who owns it.
What this sounds like to an employer

In an interview, "AI sometimes makes mistakes" is a weak answer that anyone could give. "AI hallucinates because it predicts plausible text rather than retrieving facts, so I ground it in source documents and verify every figure before anything goes to a client" is the answer that gets you hired.

★

Take away: Confident and correct are different things. Ground the model in real sources, verify every number, and never let unchecked output reach a customer.

Topic 08 of 11

Human + AI collaboration

The useful question was never "will AI replace this job". It is "which parts of this job should AI do, and which parts must stay human".

Organisations that get this split right see genuine gains. Organisations that get it wrong go one of two ways: they ban AI outright and fall behind, or they let it write to customers unchecked and pay for it publicly. Both failures come from never thinking about the division of labour.

Give to AIKeep with the human
Reading 500 documents nobody has time to readDeciding what the organisation does about them
The first draft of anythingThe final wording that carries your name
Converting messy text into structured fieldsConfirming the extraction is actually correct
Generating options and anglesChoosing, based on the relationship and the context
Consistent formatting and repetitive rewritingEthics, fairness and the judgement calls
Speed and volumeAccountability for the outcome

The working loop

1 · FrameDefine the task, the audience, the constraints and the output format you need.
→
2 · GenerateLet AI produce the draft, the extraction or the set of options.
→
3 · VerifyCheck facts, figures, tone and fit. Correct or regenerate whatever fails.
→
4 · DecideApply judgement, take the decision, own the result.

Notice that steps 1, 3 and 4 are entirely human, and that all of the professional skill lives there. Someone who only knows step 2 has learned a tool, and tools change every year. Someone who is strong at 1, 3 and 4 has learned a way of working — which is what the remaining nine modules develop.

The honest version

AI will not replace you. But a colleague who has restructured their work around AI will produce roughly three times what you produce, at comparable quality — and that comparison, not the technology, is what actually affects careers. The purpose of this program is to make sure you are that colleague.

★

Take away: AI does the reading, drafting and structuring. Humans frame the task, verify the output and own the decision. Frame → Generate → Verify → Decide.

Topic 09 of 11 · Case & activity

500 customer enquiries every month

Everything so far, applied to one ordinary business problem — then handed to you to do yourself.

A mid-sized technology company receives roughly 500 enquiries a month through its website, email and trade-show contacts. They arrive as free text — some detailed, some a single line, and some not sales enquiries at all.

Traditional approach

One executive, manually

  1. Opens and reads every enquiry
  2. Works out what the customer wants
  3. Categorises it by hand
  4. Assigns it to a salesperson
  5. Types a short summary — when there is time
AI-assisted approach

AI first pass, human review

  1. AI reads the enquiry
  2. Identifies the actual requirement
  3. Scores it Hot / Warm / Cold
  4. Extracts company, budget, requirement, timeline
  5. Writes a briefing for the salesperson
500enquiries per month
~6 minmanual handling, each
50 hrsper month, manual
~8 hrswith AI, review only

Indicative figures for discussion. The point is not the exact saving — it is that the executive moves from processing enquiries to acting on the ones that matter.

The real benefit is not the time saved

When a human handles 500 enquiries manually, the last hundred receive less attention than the first. Categorisation drifts with mood and workload. Enquiries arriving on a busy day sit unread. Good leads go cold silently, and nobody can see it happening.

  • Consistency. Enquiry 500 is assessed by the same standard as enquiry 1.
  • Structure. Free text becomes fields, so the pipeline becomes measurable and reportable.
  • Speed. Hot leads surface within minutes rather than days — and response time is the strongest single predictor of B2B conversion.
Where the human stays

AI proposes the category and drafts the summary. A salesperson still checks the Hot leads before acting, because a misread budget or a missed nuance is a lost deal. The AI is doing the reading, not the selling.

Your practical activity

Below are ten enquiries of the kind this company actually receives. Work through them with an AI tool. Not all of them are sales leads — noticing that is part of the exercise.

#Enquiry as received
1"We are a manufacturing unit in Pune, around 120 staff. Current system is Tally and it is not handling production planning. Need ERP with production and quality modules. Budget approx 8–10 lakh. Want to go live before the next financial year."
2"Sir please send me the price list."
3"I am a final year B.Tech student. Do you offer internships in your development team?"
4"Real estate developer, Nashik. Sales team of 25 people, currently on spreadsheets. Looking at CRM. We are evaluating three vendors and will decide within three weeks. Can you do a demo on Thursday?"
5"Our GST report is showing wrong figures for last month. This is urgent, our filing is due. Ticket number GT-4412."
6"IT services company, about 400 employees. Thinking about an HRMS at some point, probably next financial year. Just want to understand what is available. No budget approved yet."
7"Pharma distribution, Mumbai. We need batch-wise inventory with expiry tracking — we are losing stock to expiry every quarter. Around 15 lakh approved. Need this running in 6 weeks."
8"Textile SME in Surat. We implemented an ERP two years ago, it never worked properly and we want to migrate. 60 users. Budget around 12 lakh, planning for Q3."
9"Hi, I have 6 years experience in ERP implementation. Attaching my CV, please consider me for any suitable opening."
10"Auto component supplier, Chakan. Need ERP with quality module for our IATF audit. Audit is in four months. Please send a proposal and arrange a demo next week."

Your four tasks

  1. Categorise each enquiry. Sales lead, support request, careers, or not relevant. Three of the ten are not sales leads — find them.
  2. Extract the requirement. For every genuine lead, pull out company type, location, product needed, user count, budget and timeline. Mark anything not stated as "not stated" — do not let the AI fill the gap with an invented value.
  3. Assign a priority. Hot, Warm or Cold, with a one-line reason. Priority should reflect budget clarity, timeline urgency and how specific the requirement is.
  4. Write the sales summary. For the Hot leads, produce a three-line briefing a salesperson could read thirty seconds before calling: what they need, why now, and how to open the conversation.
The trap to watch for

Enquiry 2 gives the AI almost nothing. Many models will still confidently produce a company size and an industry for it. Check your output for exactly this — the moment you catch the model inventing a field, Topic 07 has stopped being theory.

Where this applies

This one workflow — read unstructured input, classify it, extract fields, summarise for a human — sits underneath a large share of business AI. You meet it again in Sales as lead qualification, in Support as ticket triage, in HR as resume screening, and in Operations as purchase-order processing. Learn it properly here and the later modules become variations on a pattern you already know.

★

Take away: AI reads and structures at volume with perfect consistency. The human still decides — and still catches what the model invented.
