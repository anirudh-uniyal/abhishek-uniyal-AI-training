01

What is a prompt?

A prompt is the complete instruction you give an AI system. Most people write prompts the way they type into a search engine — a few keywords, a short question — and then conclude that AI output is generic. The output is generic because the brief was.

The mental shift that makes everything else work: stop treating AI like a search box and start treating it like a capable new colleague on their first day. Very intelligent, very fast, extremely well read — but they have never met your customers, never seen your product, and do not know what a good answer looks like in your company. You would never hand that person a two-word instruction and expect usable work.

Key idea

The model is the same for you and your competitor. The instruction is not. In a workplace where everyone has the same tools, the prompt is the differentiator — which is why this is a hiring-relevant skill, not a software trick.

Why "prompt engineering" and not just "asking"

Engineering implies something deliberate and repeatable. Anyone can get a good answer once by luck. A professional writes an instruction that produces a good answer reliably, on inputs they have not seen yet — so it can be handed to a colleague, run on 200 records, or built into a process. That reliability is the whole discipline.

02

The six parts of a professional prompt

Almost every strong business prompt contains the same six components. Learn them as a checklist and the quality of your output stops depending on inspiration.

ComponentWhat it doesExample fragment
RoleSets the perspective, vocabulary and priorities of the answer"Act as a B2B SaaS marketing manager"
ObjectiveStates the actual deliverable, not the topic"Create a 150-word introduction email"
AudienceDetermines tone, depth and what to leave out"for an HR manager at a mid-sized company"
ContextSupplies the facts the model cannot know"our product is an AI employee training platform"
ConstraintsBounds length, tone, language and what to avoid"professional and persuasive, no jargon, invent no statistics"
Output formatMakes the result usable without reformatting"three business benefits and one call to action"

Not every prompt needs all six. A quick internal summary needs two or three. But the rule scales cleanly: the higher the stakes, the more of the six you specify. Anything going to a customer should have all six.

Portfolio note

By the end of this program you will hand over a Prompt Engineering Workbook of 20–30 professional prompts. Start collecting from this module — every prompt you write that works well is an asset you reuse, and it is the most concrete thing you can show an interviewer.

03

Role prompting

Telling the model who to be changes what it produces. Ask for feedback on a proposal as "a procurement head" and you get pricing and risk. Ask as "a technical architect" and you get integration and scalability. Same document, different lens.

Why it works comes straight from Module 1: the model predicts the most probable continuation. Assigning a role narrows the field to text that a person in that role would plausibly write, which pulls the vocabulary, the assumptions and the priorities along with it.

Specific roles beat impressive ones

Weak role

Too broad to change anything

  1. "Act as a marketing expert"
  2. "You are an AI assistant"
  3. "Act as a business consultant"
Strong role

Narrow enough to shift the output

  1. "Act as a B2B marketing manager selling ERP software to Indian manufacturing SMEs"
  2. "Act as a CFO reviewing this proposal for hidden recurring costs"
  3. "Act as a sceptical buyer who has already been let down by one ERP vendor"
What a role does not do

Calling the model a chartered accountant does not give it accounting knowledge it lacks, and it does not make it accurate. Role changes framing and style, not facts. The verification rules from Module 1 apply exactly as before.

04

Context — the single biggest lever

If you improve only one thing about your prompting, improve this. Module 1 established that the model knows nothing about your company beyond what you put in the context window. Most disappointing AI output is not a model failure — it is a briefing failure.

What counts as context

  • The product or service — what it actually does, in your words.
  • The customer — industry, size, role, what they care about, what they fear.
  • The situation — first outreach, follow-up after a demo, recovering a stalled deal.
  • The source material — the actual email thread, the real price list, the real dataset.
  • What has already been tried — so it does not hand you back what failed last month.
The test to apply

If a new employee would need to be told something before doing this task, the model needs to be told it too. Everything you leave out, the model fills in with a generic average — or invents.

Grounding beats recall

There is a large difference between asking the model what it remembers and giving it the source to work from. A prompt that says "Based only on the pricing document below, answer the customer question" hallucinates far less than one that says "What does our pricing look like?". Whenever the real document exists, paste it in and instruct the model to use only that.

Grounded prompt pattern
Using ONLY the information in the document below, answer the customer question.
If the document does not contain the answer, reply exactly: "Not covered in the
document" — do not use outside knowledge and do not guess.

DOCUMENT:
"""
[paste the real pricing sheet, policy or specification here]
"""

CUSTOMER QUESTION:
"""
[paste the customer email here]
"""

This one pattern will prevent most of the hallucination problems from Module 1. Keep it in your workbook.

05

Constraints

Constraints are what turn a passable answer into one you can use without rewriting. Without them the model defaults to a safe average: medium length, neutral tone, faintly American, slightly over-explained.

Constraint typeExampleWhy it matters
Length"Maximum 150 words", "exactly 5 bullet points"Length drives whether it gets read at all
Tone"Professional and persuasive, not salesy"Wrong tone loses the reader in one line
Language"Plain business English, Indian context, amounts in rupees"Defaults are usually American
Must include"Include three business benefits and one call to action"Guarantees the elements you are accountable for
Must avoid"No jargon, no superlatives, no emoji"Removes the tells that make text read as AI-written
Honesty"Do not invent any statistics, names or case studies"Directly reduces hallucination risk
The one constraint to add to everything

"If you do not have enough information to answer accurately, ask me a question instead of guessing." This single line converts silent invention into a visible question — and a question you can answer is far cheaper than a wrong number you did not notice.

06

Output formats

Specifying the shape of the answer is what lets AI output slot into real work instead of sitting in a chat window. Prose has to be re-read and reformatted by a human. A table can be pasted into a deck, sorted, compared and reviewed at a glance.

  • Table — for anything comparative: leads, vendors, candidates, options.
  • Numbered list — for steps, priorities and recommendations.
  • Structured sections — "Summary / Findings / Risks / Recommendation".
  • Email — with subject line specified separately.
  • JSON or CSV — when the output feeds a system rather than a person.
Specify the columns, not just "a table"
Return the result as a markdown table with exactly these columns:

Enquiry no. | Type | Company | Requirement | Budget | Timeline | Priority | Reason

Rules:
- One row per enquiry, in the original order.
- Where a field was not stated, write "Not stated". Never infer or estimate it.
- Priority must be exactly one of: Hot, Warm, Cold.
- Keep Reason to a single short sentence.

Notice what those four rules do: they make the output comparable across runs. That is the difference between a chat answer and something you can build a process on — and it is exactly the format Module 1's enquiry activity asked for.

07

Zero-shot and few-shot prompting

The terms sound academic; the distinction is simply whether you show examples.

Zero-shot

You describe the task and give no examples. This is most of everyday use, and for standard tasks — summarise this, draft that, explain this — it is entirely sufficient.

Few-shot

You include two to five worked examples of input and the output you want, then give the real input. The model infers the pattern from your examples rather than from your description.

Few-shot lead categorisation
Categorise each enquiry as Hot, Warm or Cold, following these examples exactly.

Enquiry: "Need ERP for 120-staff manufacturing unit, budget 8-10 lakh, want to
go live before financial year end."
Output: Hot — clear budget, defined requirement, hard deadline.

Enquiry: "Thinking about an HRMS at some point next year. No budget approved."
Output: Warm — real need, but no budget and no timeline.

Enquiry: "Please send price list."
Output: Cold — no company, requirement, budget or timeline stated.

Now categorise this enquiry in the same format:
Enquiry: "[paste the new enquiry here]"
Zero-shotFew-shot
You provideA description of the taskWorked examples of the task
Best forCommon tasks with an obvious right shapeCompany-specific style, scoring or judgement
ConsistencyGoodNoticeably better across many inputs
EffortSecondsSome setup, then reusable forever
When it actually pays off

Few-shot earns its setup cost the moment a task repeats. Categorising one enquiry — zero-shot is fine. Categorising 500 the same way every month, so the results are comparable and can be reported on — few-shot, and it becomes a company asset rather than a personal trick.

08

Chain-of-thought

For anything with multiple steps or competing factors, asking the model to reason before concluding produces markedly better answers. The high-level idea is enough for business use: make the intermediate thinking explicit instead of jumping to a verdict.

The mechanism follows from Module 1. The model predicts each next token from what is already in the context. If the reasoning steps are written out, they become part of that context and the conclusion is built on them. If it answers immediately, there are no steps to build on.

Structured reasoning, then the answer
Before recommending anything, work through this in order:

1. List the factors that matter for this decision.
2. For each factor, state what the data below actually shows.
3. Note where the data is missing or too weak to conclude from.
4. Only then give your recommendation, and say which factor drove it.

Do not begin with the recommendation.

Step 3 is the one people leave out, and it is the most valuable. It converts a confident guess into an explicit list of what you still need to find out.

Reasoning shown is not reasoning verified

A model can produce well-structured, entirely wrong reasoning. The steps make it easier for you to check, which is the real benefit — you can see where the logic went wrong instead of only seeing a wrong conclusion. It is not a substitute for checking.

09

Iterative prompting

Beginners write one prompt, get a mediocre answer, and conclude AI is overrated. Professionals expect the first output to be a starting point. Prompting is a conversation, not a command.

The refinement moves

Problem with the draftWhat you say next
Too long or too dense"Cut this to 120 words. Keep the three benefits, drop the background."
Tone is wrong"Too salesy. Rewrite it as a peer talking to a peer."
Too generic"This could be about any product. Rewrite using only the specifics I gave you."
Missed something"You did not address the pricing objection. Add a short paragraph on it."
You want choice"Give me three versions with different opening lines."
Wrong reader"Now rewrite the same content for a CFO instead of an HR manager."

The self-critique loop

The strongest single refinement technique is to make the model review its own work against the brief before you do:

Two-step critique
Step 1 — Review your draft against my original brief. List every point where it
is weak, generic, unsupported by the context I gave you, or missing something
I asked for. Be specific and do not defend the draft.

Step 2 — Rewrite the draft fixing everything you listed.
Close the loop

When an iteration finally produces what you wanted, fold the correction back into the original prompt. Do not keep re-explaining the same thing in every conversation — that is how a one-off chat becomes a reusable prompt.

10

Evaluating a prompt

"It gave a good answer" is not evaluation — it might have been luck. A prompt is an asset only if it performs on inputs you have not seen yet.

TestQuestion to ask
ObjectiveDid it produce the deliverable I actually asked for, or just text about the topic?
AccuracyAre the facts, figures and names correct and checkable?
GroundingDid it stay inside the context I supplied, or drift into invention?
Audience fitWould the intended reader find the tone and depth right?
UsabilityCan I use it with light edits, or am I effectively rewriting it?
RepeatabilityDoes it work on three different inputs, not just the one I tested?
The professional standard

Repeatability is the test that separates a lucky chat from an engineered prompt. Before you put a prompt in your workbook, run it on three genuinely different inputs. If the quality holds, you have built something. If it collapses on input two, your prompt was carrying assumptions that only applied to input one.

Worked example

The same task, briefed two ways

Here is the difference the six components make on one ordinary request.

Basic prompt
Write a marketing email.

This produces a competent, entirely generic email about a product the model has invented, for a reader it has guessed at. Nothing about it is usable.

Professional prompt
Act as a B2B SaaS marketing manager. Create a 150-word email for an HR manager
introducing an AI-based employee training platform. The tone should be
professional and persuasive. Include three business benefits and one call to
action.
ComponentWhere it appearsWhat it changed
Role"Act as a B2B SaaS marketing manager"Business-to-business framing rather than consumer copy
Objective"Create an email introducing…"A first-touch introduction, not a renewal or a newsletter
Audience"for an HR manager"Speaks to training outcomes and admin load, not to a CTO
Context"an AI-based employee training platform"Gives the model an actual product to write about
Constraints"150 words, professional and persuasive"Short enough to be read, right register for a cold email
Output format"three business benefits and one call to action"Guarantees the structural elements the email needs to work
› Before you read on — what is still missing from the professional prompt? Think first

It is a good prompt, and it is still improvable. Three gaps worth noticing:

  • No honesty constraint. Nothing stops the model inventing statistics, client names or case studies to make the benefits sound stronger.
  • No subject line. The single most important part of a cold email is not requested, so you will get a body and have to write the subject yourself.
  • Thin context. "An AI-based employee training platform" is a category, not a product. What does it actually do, and who is it better than?

Catching gaps like these in your own prompts is the skill Section 10 is testing. A good prompt is rarely the first one you write.
